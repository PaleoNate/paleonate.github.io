Putting a number on fossil lianas

A calculator for estimating how likely it is that a piece of fossil wood came from a climbing plant.

Nathan Jud · November 1, 2026 · with Julieta Rosell and Mark Olson

Lianas and other climbing plants reach the forest canopy by climbing on other plants. In this way, they can grow up into the canopy quickly instead of investing their resources into a thick trunk. That strategy is evident in the wood. The vessels, the conduits that carry water, tend to appear wide compared to those in a tree branch of the same diameter, and many climbers also have cambial variants that keep the stem flexible and help it withstand being twisted.

Paleobotanists have relied on these features for a long time to recognize climbers among fossil woods. However, deciding whether the vessels look wide enough to conclude the stem probably came from a climber has been a judgment call. Basically a mental comparison with all the woods we have observed or at least with closely related species. Any two paleobotanists might look at the same specimen and reach different conclusions, or have different confidence in the same conclusion.

What I find exciting is that we can now put numbers on that judgment, thanks to the excellent dataset my co-authors Julieta Rosell and Mark Olson assembled. They measured stem diameter, vessel diameter, and vessel frequency in living dicots and for each plant they also recorded the presence or absence of successive cambia (a type of cambial variant) and the growth habit: 779 species in all, 87 of them climbers (Rosell & Olson 2014). Together, we used those measurements to train a statistical model. Given the same measurements from a fossil, the model estimates the probability that the stem came from a climber, along with how confident we can be in that estimate. The full method, including a version that also considers the fossil's phylogenetic position, is in our paper coming out in New Phytologist. The calculator below uses only the wood anatomy measurements.

The model is mostly a formal version of the "wide vessels for its stem diameter" comparison. Larger stems have larger vessels in nearly every plant, so the model compares a specimen's vessels with what a typical dicot stem of the same diameter would have, and it does the same for the number of vessels per square millimeter.

Recognizing climbers in the fossil record is useful because they are closely tied to climate. Climbing plants are most diverse in the tropics, and their abundance depends on things like water availability, climate, and disturbance history. So, a reliable way to identify them in fossil wood assemblages gives us another line of evidence for reconstructing ancient environments alongside evidence from leaves and pollen.

How to measure

Measure vessels the way they were measured in the training data (Rosell & Olson 2014): in the outer wood of a stem whose diameter you know. Vessel diameter increases from the pith outward, so measurements from wood near the pith, or from fragments of unknown radial position, will underestimate vessel diameter. Treat predictions from those specimens with caution.

What the calculator does

  1. Transforms the measurements exactly as the training data were transformed. All three are log-transformed, vessel diameter and frequency are compared with what is typical for a stem of that diameter (using the regressions fitted to the training data), and everything is standardized with the training means and standard deviations. These constants come from the training data and are never refit on new specimens.
  2. Predicts from wood traits alone. The model's fixed effects are evaluated across the full posterior, which gives a distribution of probabilities rather than a single number.
  3. Leaves phylogeny out. The model in our New Phytologist paper can also place a fossil on the family tree of living plants, using its age and taxonomy, and borrow information from its relatives. This calculator uses the traits only.
  4. Makes a call. A specimen is classified as a climber (non-self-supporting) when the posterior median exceeds the threshold of 0.39, chosen to maximize F1 in cross-validation.

Reading the results

  • Report the probability and its interval, not only the class. A specimen whose interval spans the threshold is genuinely uncertain.
  • Measurements outside the training range are extrapolations, and the calculator flags them.
  • The model was trained on extant dicots with stem diameters under 50 cm. It does not apply to monocots, gymnosperms or other woody groups.

About the uncertainty. The calculator uses a thinned sample of the model's combined posterior: 4,700 draws, 50 from each of the 94 retained bootstrap replicates. The intervals therefore carry both uncertainty in the model's parameters and uncertainty from which specimen represented each species in the training data. Medians agree with the paper's to within Monte Carlo error (about ±0.01).
